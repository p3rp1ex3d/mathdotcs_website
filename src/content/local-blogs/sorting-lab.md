## What even *is* an algorithm?

For a long time, I used to think algorithms belonged only to computers.

Something mathematical.  
Something hidden inside machines.

But algorithms existed long before computers ever did.

A recipe is an algorithm.

Instructions for tying shoelaces form an algorithm too.

Even asking a friend for directions:

```txt
walk two streets forward
turn left near the bookstore
cross the signal
the café is beside the old theatre
```

is really just a sequence of ordered steps.

That is all an algorithm is:

> a methodical way of transforming an input into an output.

You begin somewhere messy —
with numbers, ingredients, information, confusion, or a problem —

and through a sequence of small decisions, arrive somewhere clearer.

---

## Small steps matter more than they seem

Imagine making tea.

Not the abstract idea of tea.

Actual tea.

You might:

1. boil water  
2. place tea leaves into a cup  
3. pour the water  
4. wait patiently  
5. add milk or sugar  

The order matters.

If the water never boils, the tea changes.

If you forget the leaves entirely, the process still *runs* —
but the result becomes meaningless.

Computers behave similarly.

They follow instructions with almost uncomfortable literalness.

Tiny changes in order can completely transform the outcome.

That is why algorithms are not merely *lists* of instructions.

They are carefully structured flows of logic.

## Algorithms quietly run the modern world

Once you begin noticing algorithms, it becomes difficult to stop seeing them.

When you:
- search online,
- scroll endlessly through recommendations,
- unlock your phone,
- navigate using maps,
- watch videos,
- or even organize photographs,

algorithms are operating beneath the surface.

Some algorithms:
- sort information,
- some search efficiently,
- some compress enormous files,
- some recognize patterns in speech or faces,
- and others calculate the shortest path between two places.

Even music recommendations are really just gigantic chains of decisions being made continuously.

Modern software is less like a single machine and more like an ecosystem of interacting algorithms.

---

## Why study them at all?

Because algorithms slowly change how you think.

You begin noticing:
- patterns,
- structure,
- repetition,
- inefficiency,
- and elegant shortcuts.

Two solutions may both work.

But one might:
- take less time,
- use less memory,
- scale to millions of people,
- or simply feel more graceful.

That is where algorithms stop feeling mechanical and start feeling almost artistic.

They are not only instructions.

They are strategies.

## Tiny toy problems become enormous systems

Most journeys into algorithms begin with tiny examples.

Sorting numbers.

Searching arrays.

Finding the largest value.

Tracing loops line by line on paper.

At first these exercises can feel strangely small.

But hidden inside them are the same ideas powering:
- search engines,
- GPS navigation,
- robotics,
- cryptography,
- recommendation systems,
- and artificial intelligence.

The scale changes dramatically.

The foundations often do not.

Break the problem into smaller repeatable steps.

That idea appears everywhere.

## A notebook of computational sketches

This series is not meant to feel like a textbook full of rigid definitions.

Think of it more like opening someone's notebook.

A place for:
- observations,
- sketches,
- experiments,
- tiny discoveries,
- and slowly evolving intuition.

We will move through algorithms visually and patiently.

Not just:
> “what does the code say?”

but also:

- why does it behave this way?
- what changes in memory?
- which values move?
- what stays fixed?
- how does the computer "think" through the problem?

We will begin with simple sorting algorithms and gradually wander toward more interesting ideas.

One careful step at a time.
